§4

The census: a PLATO thought experiment

Simulate the paper's actual two-step experiment. Planets are selected by mass (all 12 M⊕ here) and by host-star age — never by radius: radius is the measurement. Step 1, calibrate: both rival stories — all dry, or all 70% water — get their gas fraction tuned so each reproduces the same observed radii in the old (5 Gyr) sample. After that, the old sample can't tell them apart by construction. Step 2, test: the tuned stories predict different radii for young planets, because dry planets carrying more gas were puffier in the past. Observe a young batch with realistic noise (±10% on ages) and ask: can we reject the wrong story?

Concept tab · the frost line · not a paper

Alibert's whole argument rests on one boundary: inside it water is vapour and planets are born dry, outside it water is ice and planets can be born wet. That boundary is not a place. It moved by two orders of magnitude over the Sun's life, and every planet, asteroid and comet remembers where it was when their solids condensed. This tab computes the line's whole history for the solar system, on a log clock, and explains each of its seven movements.

Read the two heating terms first. Viscous heating scales as Ṁ2/5 and falls steeply with distance, as r−9/10, so it rules the inner disk while gas is still pouring through it. Irradiation of a flared disk falls slowly, as r−3/7, so it always wins far out and it becomes the only term once accretion dies. The frost line is where the sum of the two crosses 170 K. Everything in §2 is one of those two terms rising or falling, or the disk itself disappearing.

§2

Every movement, explained

The numbers in each card are computed live from the model above and follow the sliders. The physics in each card does not.

1Embedded protostar 0 – 300 kyr

–

The Sun is still gaining mass from its collapsing cloud, gas streams through the disk at a few 10−7 M☉ per year, and the friction of that flow keeps the midplane hot out to Jupiter's orbit and beyond. Accretion is not steady: pile-ups in the inner disk drain in outbursts, when the accretion luminosity briefly reaches hundreds of L☉ and the front sweeps out to 40 AU. That is what ALMA sees in V883 Ori. The first solids in meteorites, the CAIs, condense in this era, inside the line, from a gas that was hot enough to vaporise rock.

Sets the line: viscous heating, plus bursts of accretion light

2Viscous decline 300 kyr – 3 Myr

–

The infall stops and the disk drains itself. The accretion rate falls roughly as t−3/2, so the viscous term fades and the line sweeps inward through the region where the giant planets sit. Jupiter's core reaches tens of Earth masses in about 1 Myr, and meteorite isotopes show the inner and outer nebula stopped mixing at the same moment. The natural reading is that Jupiter grew at the frost line while it passed 3 to 5 AU, then acted as a dam: pebbles drifting in from the cold outer disk piled up behind it, and the inner disk stayed dry even after the line moved on. Morbidelli et al. 2016 call this the fossilised snow line.

Sets the line: viscous heating, fading

3Passive floor 3 Myr – disk dispersal

–

Once accretion drops below about 10−9 M☉ per year the disk is heated by starlight alone. A flared disk catches the Sun's light at a grazing angle of a few degrees, so its interior is only about half as warm as a bare body at the same distance, and the frost line settles inside 1 AU. This is the dry-Earth problem: if the line spent a million years at half an AU, why is Earth only a tenth of a per cent water? The answers on offer are Jupiter's dam, an inner disk whose planetesimals had already formed dry, and a dead-zone correction that keeps the line a little further out.

Sets the line: irradiation of the flared disk

4The disk disappears a few Myr, over 100 kyr

–

Far-ultraviolet and X-ray light from the Sun evaporates the last gas in about a hundred thousand years, a blink on this axis. The line does not move gradually here: it jumps. Without a disk there is no grazing-angle geometry and no shading of the midplane; a body sits in full sunlight and reaches its blackbody equilibrium temperature. The rust line is where an airless body reaches 170 K, and it sits several times further out than the shaded midplane did. From this moment on there is one frost line, and it belongs to the Sun's luminosity alone.

Sets the line: direct sunlight on bare bodies

5Hayashi dimming disk dispersal – 50 Myr

–

The young Sun is a large, cool, contracting star. As it shrinks along the Hayashi track its luminosity falls to about half the present value, reaches a minimum near 10 to 20 Myr, then recovers slightly as hydrogen burning ignites and the Sun arrives on the zero-age main sequence at about 0.7 L☉. The frost line follows the square root of the luminosity, so it dips in and drifts back out. This is when the terrestrial planets finish: Mars in the first 10 Myr, Earth's Moon-forming impact somewhere between 50 and 100 Myr. The belt records the boundary: Vesta at 2.36 AU melted dry, Ceres at 2.77 AU kept its ice.

Sets the line: the Sun's pre-main-sequence luminosity

6Main-sequence brightening 50 Myr – 10 Gyr

–

Fusion turns hydrogen into helium, the core slowly contracts and heats, and the Sun brightens by about 10 % per Gyr. The line creeps outward through the asteroid belt. Today it sits near 2.7 AU, which is why comets switch on their water activity around 3 AU and why the belt turns from dry S-types to hydrated C-types across the same distance. Within another few Gyr the line will pass Ceres. This is the only era where the frost line is something you can go and watch.

Sets the line: the Sun's slowly rising luminosity

7Giant branch 10 – 12.3 Gyr · schematic

–

With the core's hydrogen spent, the Sun swells into a red giant and its luminosity climbs to a few thousand L☉ at the tip of the giant branch. The frost line races out past Neptune and through the Kuiper belt. Every icy moon of Jupiter and Saturn, every dwarf planet, becomes a comet for a while. The habitable zone moves with it, out to tens of AU. The curve here is a sketch: the model interpolates a handful of points from stellar evolution tracks, and the details of the branch, including mass loss, are not modelled.

Sets the line: the Sun's post-main-sequence luminosity

What this means for Alibert's argument. The habitable zone of a Sun-like star spans about 0.95 to 1.7 AU. For most of the time solids were being assembled into planets, the frost line sat outside that range; it dips inside only in the last, gas-poor phase of the disk, when little solid mass is left to be redistributed. A habitable-zone planet made of tens of per cent water therefore did not condense where it orbits. It grew beyond the line and migrated, or the water was carried in. Earth's own tenth of a per cent is the delivered case. That is why a measured water fraction is a birth certificate, and why Alibert wants it.

Alibert 2016 hunted for ways to break the composition degeneracy. Dorn 2017 does the opposite: embrace it. Put priors on every interior parameter, run the forward model thousands of times, and let Bayes' theorem state exactly how much — and how little — a mass, a radius, and the star's chemistry really tell you.

Then flip on the stellar abundance constraint (Fe/Si from the host star's spectrum, the Thiabaud proxy) and watch which posteriors tighten — and which don't care.

The structural difference: in the image problem the output has far more dimensions than the parameters, so confusion is a property of the instrument's kernel and δ₁ comes out independent of flux and noise. In the interior problem the parameters outnumber the observables, so the degeneracy is structural and no signal-to-noise closes it. Alibert's degeneracy is therefore the paper's cancellation case, an exact null direction, while Dorn's posterior width is its contamination case, finite confusion set by noise. §4

The uncorrelation age

The paper's uncorrelation time is how long to wait before a second observation is independent of the first, because the planets have moved. The interior analogue is age. The truth P and its impostor Q* agree at 5 Gyr by construction, but they cool at different rates, so the cost between them grows toward younger ages. The question becomes: at what age, and with how many planets, does J(P(t), Q*(t)) cross the threshold? Q* is the two-layer story chosen in §2. Concept tab · atmospheric escape · not a paper

Kubyshkina's population is carved by one family of processes — hydrodynamic escape, fed by stellar XUV and by the planet's own leftover heat. But gas leaves planets in many more ways, and the same word “escape” hides very different physics: a statistical tail, a fluid wind, a chemical kick, a plasma sweep, a hammer blow. This tab lays them side by side, then lets you play with the three that a formula can capture.

§1

Eight ways to lose an atmosphere

Sort them by what supplies the energy. Thermal processes run on the gas temperature; stellar processes on photons or plasma from the star; the rest on collisions or tides. The badge on each card says whether Kubyshkina & Fossati (2022) include it.

Jeans escape thermalnot in K&F 2022

At the exobase — the height where collisions stop — the fastest molecules in the Maxwell–Boltzmann tail exceed the escape speed and leave one by one. The flux scales as (1+λ)e−λ with λ = GMm/(kTr), so it is exquisitely sensitive to particle mass and gravity: hydrogen from small, warm bodies leaks; everything heavier stays.

Where · H from Earth (~¼ of its hydrogen loss), Mars, Titan's H₂ · negligible for H/He envelopes with λ ≳ 30

XUV-driven hydrodynamic escape stellarin K&F 2022

X-ray and extreme-UV photons are absorbed high up, heat the gas to ~10⁴ K, and the whole upper atmosphere expands outward as a transonic wind — a fluid, not a tail, and species do not need to be fast individually, they are carried. In the energy-limited approximation Ṁ ≈ επFXUVR³/(GM); at very high flux radiative cooling caps it below that.

Where · hot Jupiters (HD 209458 b's Lyα tail), sub-Neptunes, early Venus and Earth under the young Sun

Boil-off / core-powered escape thermalin K&F 2022

A freshly formed planet is puffy and hot; if its gravity is too weak for its temperature — Λ = GMmH/(kTeqR) ≲ 15–25 — the envelope flows out hydrodynamically without XUV, powered by the cooling luminosity of the interior and the star's bolometric light. It is the fluid limit that Jeans escape tends to when λ → few, and it lasts only the first tens of Myr — but it sets the starting point for everything after.

Where · every low-mass planet in the first 50 Myr · the reason K&F's youngest sub-Neptunes shrink so fast

Photochemical escape stellar · chemistrynot in K&F 2022

An exothermic reaction hands a single atom more than its escape energy: dissociative recombination O₂⁺ + e⁻ → O + O releases up to 7 eV, enough to launch oxygen off Mars. Needs no hot exobase and is species-selective — it removes heavy atoms that Jeans never could.

Where · Mars's dominant oxygen loss today · Venus (O, C) · Titan (N)

Ion pickup & sputtering stellar windnot in K&F 2022

Atoms ionised above the exobase are swept away by the stellar wind's motional electric field (pickup); some are slammed back into the atmosphere and knock neutrals out (sputtering). Decisive for unmagnetised Mars and Venus; a magnetic field redirects rather than stops it, and can even feed escape along open field lines.

Where · Mars (MAVEN measures ~2–3 kg/s of ions leaving) · Venus · likely close-in rocky exoplanets around M dwarfs

Charge exchange & polar wind plasmanot in K&F 2022

Inside a magnetosphere, hot plasma ions swap an electron with cold neutral atoms, producing fast neutrals that are no longer confined by the field and escape. Along open polar field lines, light ions (H⁺, He⁺, O⁺) are accelerated outward by the ambipolar electric field — the polar wind.

Where · Earth loses a few kg/s of hydrogen this way — more than by Jeans · Saturn, Jupiter

Impact erosion collisionalnot in K&F 2022

A large impactor's vapour plume drives away all atmosphere above the tangent plane of the impact site; giant impacts can eject a sizeable fraction globally through the ground shock. Dominant during accretion and heavy bombardment, and it does not care about particle mass at all.

Where · Mars and the Moon-forming impact · a competing explanation for thin atmospheres on small bodies

Roche-lobe overflow tidalnot in K&F 2022

For ultra-short-period planets the star's gravity truncates the atmosphere at the Roche lobe; any gas that expands beyond it belongs to the star and streams away through the L1 point. This is how runaway inflation ends and why hot Jupiters have a maximum radius near the Roche limit.

Where · WASP-12 b, KELT-9 b · the end state of the most irradiated giants

The Jeans-vs-thermal confusion, settled: Jeans escape and boil-off both run on the gas's own temperature. The difference is how the gas leaves — Jeans is a statistical leak from the tail of the velocity distribution (collisionless, one particle at a time, only when λ is large); boil-off is the fluid limit where λ has dropped so low that the bulk atmosphere itself flows outward. Kubyshkina's models include the fluid case and skip the tail — because for H/He envelopes on planets heavy enough to matter, the tail is empty (see §2).

§1

The polytrope

Hydrostatic equilibrium plus a polytropic equation of state P = Kρ1+1/n collapses all of stellar structure into one dimensionless ODE: y'' + (2/x)y' + yⁿ = 0, with y(0)=1, y'(0)=0. The solution y is (a power of) the density profile; where y first hits zero is the star's surface. Exact solutions exist only for n = 0, 1, 5 — and note the 2/x term: the equation is singular at the very center, which is what makes it a classic numerical test case.

§7

Many small networks beat one big one

The FBPINN idea: cut the domain into overlapping windows, give each window its own tiny network that sees only its window rescaled to [−1, 1], and sum them with smooth window functions. Inside its window every subnetwork faces a low-frequency problem, so spectral bias never bites, and the sum is smooth by construction, so no interface loss is needed. §3

A chain of Laplace resonances

Consecutive pairs sit just outside first-order mean-motion resonances: c–d near 2:1, d–e and e–f near 3:2, f–g near 4:3. Each pair's distance from exact resonance sets a super-period, and the remarkable thing is that all four super-periods are the same, about 260 days. That coincidence is what a Laplace resonance means: the three-body angles ψ built from consecutive triplets stop circulating and librate around fixed values. §4

Transit timing variations

Planets near resonance tug on each other in step, so their transits arrive early and late with a period equal to the super-period, about 260 days here, and amplitudes that scale with the perturber's mass. The same N-body run, extended to six years, gives the transit times; subtracting a linear ephemeris leaves the variations. §2

Why the middle empties

Take a rocky core and dress it in a hydrogen envelope. The radius rises steeply with the envelope fraction: one per cent of the mass in H/He adds about half an Earth radius, and three per cent nearly doubles the planet. Now compute how long that envelope survives under the young star's XUV. The mass to remove grows with the envelope, but the cross-section to the star and the leverage of weak gravity at large radius grow faster at first. The survival time is t ∝ f · M² / (FXUV R(f)³), and it has a maximum. Envelopes thinner than that are lost faster than thicker ones: a planet that starts in the thin regime slides all the way to bare, and a planet above it keeps most of what it has.

§1

The pipeline, one stage at a time

plaNETic is a full-grid acceptance-rejection sampler wrapped around a neural surrogate of the BICEPS structure code. Click a stage, or step through them, to see what happens to a single synthetic planet on its way from the priors to the posterior.

Why a grid and not a chain: Markov chains and nested sampling exist to economise on forward-model calls. Once a call costs 35 microseconds instead of 14 seconds, economy is pointless, and a brute-force grid has two advantages a chain lacks: it never gets stuck, and every draw is independent, so the accepted set is the posterior without burn-in or autocorrelation bookkeeping. The price is a hundred million evaluations per planet, which the network makes affordable.

§2

The prior simplex

Core, mantle and water fractions must add to one, so a composition is a point on a triangle. plaNETic draws points uniformly on it, then applies the physics. The water prior decides which part of the triangle exists at all: born outside the iceline, water up to 50 %; born inside, water zero and the triangle collapses to its core–mantle edge. The Si/Mg/Fe prior then asks whether a mantle can be built from what the core left over: given the bulk Si/Fe and Mg/Fe, the mantle's iron fraction follows analytically, and if it comes out negative or the silicon and magnesium do not fit, the draw is rejected. That rejection is the whole reason the core posterior has the shape it has.

§4

§2

Rung 1 · the ellipse, and three periods instead of one

An eccentric orbit on its own still has one constant period. But real ellipses turn: their long axis precesses, pushed by general relativity, by the star's equatorial bulge, by the tidal bulge the star raises on the planet, and by other planets. Once the ellipse turns, three periods separate. The sidereal period is the time to come back to the same direction in space. The anomalistic period is periastron to periastron. The transit period is what a telescope measures, and it oscillates around the sidereal value with the precession period, by an amount set by the eccentricity.

What to take from this rung. Precession changes the transit period in a purely periodic way, and only when the eccentricity is not zero: at e = 0 the ellipse has no long axis to turn. For a lone planet the rates are slow, general relativity gives cycles of thousands of years and the stellar bulge is slower still, so over a decade of monitoring the signature is a gentle bend of a few seconds. Add a neighbour and the rate jumps by orders of magnitude: that is the bridge to the next rung. The hot-Jupiter preset shows the one case where the planet's own tidal bulge wins.

Rung 2 · a second planet

With two planets the problem has no closed solution, so this station integrates it: the same N-body engine as the Leleu tab, with eccentric starting orbits. Two things appear. Near a first-order resonance the planets pull in step and the transit times swing with the super-period, by an amount that grows with the neighbour's mass and with closeness to exact resonance. Inside the resonance the swing becomes a libration. And if the planets are massive and close enough that resonances overlap, the motion turns chaotic and the period never settles.

Reading the swing. The analytic formula of Lithwick, Xie and Wu says the inner planet's timing swing is the outer planet's mass ratio times the period, divided by the fractional distance from resonance. Halve the distance and the swing doubles, which is why chains like TOI-178, with every pair within one or two percent of resonance, have timing swings of tens of minutes from planets of a few Earth masses. The period itself wobbles by the swing times two pi times the period over the super-period: minutes, reversing every few months. The packed preset shows the other regime: no super-period, no repeating pattern, a diffusion index that says the period is random-walking.

§4

Rung 3 · dissipation: tides and mass loss

Everything so far conserves energy, so every change reverses. Tides do not. The bulge a planet raises on its star lags, and the lag drains orbital energy: for a hot Jupiter the orbit shrinks and the period decreases steadily, which shows in the timing plot as a parabola. WASP-12b is the measured case, 29 milliseconds per year. For small planets in a chain the same dissipation, acting through the planets' eccentricities, pushes each pair slowly away from exact resonance, which is why every pair in TOI-178 sits slightly wide of it. Mass loss, from the star or the planet, is the third dissipative term and the smallest.

Two regimes of the same physics. Tides raised on the star by a Jupiter-mass planet at one day are strong enough to spiral it in within a few million years, and the period decrease is measurable within a decade. Tides raised on an Earth-mass planet by its star are far too weak to move the orbit, but they damp the planet's eccentricity on gigayear timescales, and in a resonant pair the damped eccentricity is replaced by a small outward drift of the period ratio. That is the mechanism the Leleu paper invokes for planet b, 1.5 percent wide of the 5:3 with c. Mass loss is included for completeness: seconds to a minute over the age of the system for the star's wind, microseconds for the planet's own evaporation.

§5

Rung 4 · a distant third body

A companion far outside the transiting planet does two very different things. It makes the star orbit the common centre of mass, so the light from every transit arrives early or late by the travel time across that orbit: the period appears to change while the orbit itself does not. And if the companion is inclined, it drives Kozai-Lidov cycles that trade the inner planet's eccentricity against its inclination over long timescales, while the semi-major axis and the period stay fixed.

Apparent versus real. The light-travel-time effect is the cleanest example of a period change that is not one: a Jupiter at 5 AU moves the star by a few light-seconds over twelve years, and a naive fit would report a period drifting by hundredths of a second. It is distinguished from real effects because it is identical for every planet in the system and because transit durations do not change. Kozai cycles are the opposite case: a large real change in the orbit's shape and tilt with no change in period, because the perturbation is too slow to exchange energy. For TOI-178 neither applies, no companion is known, and a strongly inclined one would have broken the coplanarity of §6 in the Leleu tab.

§6

Rung 5 · the disk: the only time periods really changed

In the first few million years a planet sits in a gas disk, and the gravitational torque of the spiral wakes it raises drives it inward: type I migration. The rate scales with the planet's mass and the local disk mass, so two planets migrate at different speeds, converge, and when their period ratio reaches a first-order resonance slowly enough, lock. From then on they migrate together, and a third, fourth and fifth planet can join in turn. This is the rung that changes periods by orders of magnitude, and the rung that built the TOI-178 chain. Why the chain is a fossil. At full strength type I migration empties the inner disk in tens of thousands of years, far faster than disks live, which is the classic problem the efficiency slider parameterises. Whatever the true rate, capture needs convergence to be slow compared with the resonant libration, so heavy, fast pairs skip the 2:1 and lock at 3:2 or 4:3, while light, slow pairs stop at 2:1. TOI-178's 2:1, 3:2, 3:2, 4:3 sequence is therefore a record of relative migration speeds seven billion years ago, and the disk's inner edge, where migration halts, is where the chain was anchored. After the disk is gone nothing on the rungs above can undo it.

The ladder in one sentence.
